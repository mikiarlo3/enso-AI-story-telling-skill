# Guided mode — help the user find their unique angle

This is a conversation protocol, not a writing technique. Follow it when the user wants to be guided: they picked a storytelling type from the menu, asked for help finding their story, or arrived with a topic but no angle. The goal is not to write *a* piece — it's to find the angle only this person can write, then build the piece around it.

## Ground rules

- **One step at a time.** Never show the user this whole process. They see one short message per turn: a menu, a couple of questions, or a draft.
- **Numbered options, always escapable.** Every choice is a short numbered list ending with an invitation to answer in their own words. Typing "3" must always work.
- **They talk more than you.** In discovery, your messages are 2–4 sentences plus questions. Save the prose for drafts.
- **Mine everything.** Every reply contains raw material — an exact phrase, a number, a name, a buried resentment. Echo their own words back in angles and drafts; the piece should sound like them, sharpened.
- **Never draft the full piece before the angle is confirmed.** Hook first, reaction, then draft.
- **Two rounds of questions maximum** before pitching angles. A guided experience that feels like a form is worse than no guidance.

## What a unique angle is

An angle is unique when it passes all three tests:

1. **Only-you** — built on a specific moment, number, name, or detail no one else has. "The intern refreshed the dashboard forty times," not "we worked hard."
2. **Surprise** — it contradicts what the reader expects, or what everyone else in the field keeps saying (Bernbach, Kennedy). If ten competitors could open the same way, it's not an angle.
3. **Reader relevance** — it lands on something the reader already wants, fears, or suspects (Schwartz's awareness stages). A unique story nobody needed is a diary entry.

Every angle you pitch must name the moment, the surprise, and who it's for.

## Step 1 — The menu

When entering guided mode, send a short welcome (one line, no hype) and this menu, adapted to anything the user already said — if they've clearly told you the type, skip the menu and confirm it in passing instead of asking again.

> What are we telling?
> 1. **Personal or founder story** — how you got here, a lesson, an origin
> 2. **Persuade** — a talk, pitch, keynote, or manifesto
> 3. **Sell** — a sales page, ad, landing page, or email
> 4. **Brand story** — why the company exists, told so people care
> 5. **Grow an audience** — LinkedIn/social posts, a newsletter voice
> 6. **Prove it works** — a case study or customer story
> 7. **Fiction** — a short story or narrative scene
> 8. **Not sure — interview me and tell me what my story is**
>
> Pick a number or just describe it.

## Step 2 — The angle interview

Ask **2–3 questions per message, at most two rounds**. Pick from the track below for the chosen type, and adapt to what they've already told you — never ask something their earlier messages already answered. If an answer is abstract ("we had communication problems"), push once for the concrete version ("what did that look like on the worst day — who said what?").

**Track A — Moment questions** (types 1, 6, 8; Dicks):
- What's a small, specific moment from this you still think about — a sentence someone said, a 2 a.m. something, a thing you kept?
- What did you believe before, and what do you believe now? What flipped it?
- What were you sure was going to happen right before it didn't?

**Track B — Belief questions** (types 2, 4, 5; Sinek, Bernbach, Godin):
- What does everyone in your field keep saying that makes you roll your eyes?
- What do you know from doing this that outsiders always get wrong?
- If you could make your reader believe exactly one new thing, what is it — in one sentence?

**Track C — Reader questions** (types 3, 5, 6; Schwartz, Hopkins):
- Who exactly is reading, and what have they already tried that didn't work?
- Do they know this kind of solution exists, or do they only feel the problem? (Sets the awareness stage.)
- What's the number, result, or exact detail that would make them stop scrolling — the one you can actually back up?

**Track D — Fiction questions** (type 7; King):
- What's the "what if" situation? (King: situation first, plot never.)
- Whose ordinary day does it interrupt?
- What's the last image you want the reader left with?

Type 8 ("interview me") runs Track A alone, then you *tell them* which type their material wants to be, and confirm.

## Step 3 — Pitch three angles

From the interview, pitch **exactly three** candidate angles. Each is three lines:

> **1. "The angle name"** — one-sentence angle statement (the moment + the surprise).
> *Opens:* the actual first line it would open with.
> *For:* who this lands on and why.

Make the three genuinely different — typically one built on the moment (Track A material), one on the contrarian belief (Track B), one on the reader's pain (Track C) — not three phrasings of one idea. Then:

> Pick one, combine two, or tell me what's missing and I'll pitch again.

If none land after a second pitch, ask the single question you're missing rather than generating variations blind.

## Step 4 — Hook first, then draft

When an angle is chosen, load the masters for the type (table in Step 1; 2–3 files, no more) and draft **only the opening** — 2–4 sentences, in medias res, no throat-clearing. Ask: "This is the door into the piece. Walk through it, or should I cut a different one?"

On approval, write the full draft using the core workflow in SKILL.md: shape from the Step 1 table, causality between every beat, specifics from the interview verbatim, moment of change near the end, lesson after the story.

## Step 5 — The iteration loop

After **every** draft, append this menu (trim options that don't apply; renumber):

> What should change?
> 1. **Sharpen the hook** — cut deeper into the action (Sinek, Stanton)
> 2. **Raise the stakes** — swap abstractions for specifics; I may ask you for one detail (Ogilvy, Dicks)
> 3. **Cut the fat** — shorter, faster, every sentence earns the next (Sugarman, Hemingway)
> 4. **Change the voice** — name a master (Halbert's swagger, Twain's wit, Obama's cadence…) or a register
> 5. **More you** — more vulnerable, more first-person, more of your exact words (Winfrey, Dicks)
> 6. **More contrarian** — push the surprise harder (Bernbach, Kennedy)
> 7. **Fix the ending** — slow down at the change, land it later (Dicks' hourglass)
> 8. **Ship it** — run the final edit pass
>
> Number, several numbers, or just tell me what's off.

Rules for the loop:
- Apply the change and show the new draft — don't describe what you would change.
- When they pick 2 or 5 and you're out of material, ask for **one** specific detail, not another interview round.
- If they pick 4, load that master's reference file before rewriting.
- Track the round count. After three rounds without a pick of "ship it," offer a diagnosis: name the one thing you think is still weak, fix it, and recommend shipping.

## Step 6 — Ship it, keep the angle

On "ship it": run the SKILL.md edit-pass checklist silently, fix what fails, deliver the final version. Then close with their **angle statement** — one reusable line:

> **Your angle:** [the moment/belief] + [the surprise] + [who it's for].
> This is yours now — it works for the next ten pieces, not just this one. Want to spin a second format from it (LinkedIn post → talk open → email)?

That last offer is the payoff of guided mode: the user leaves with an angle they own, not just one piece of content.
