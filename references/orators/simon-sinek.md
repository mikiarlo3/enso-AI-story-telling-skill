# Simon Sinek — Openings & Purpose

Author of *Start with Why* and one of the most-watched TED speakers, known equally for purpose-driven messaging and ruthlessly efficient story openings. His philosophy: people are hooked by unresolved action and moved by purpose — so start inside the story and lead with why you do it.

## Delete your introduction
Write the introduction, then cut it and open with what was paragraph two. First paragraphs are almost always warm-up — context, credentials, "in today's world" framing — written for the writer's comfort, not the reader's. The second paragraph is usually where something actually happens, and readers decide whether to continue within seconds.
**When to use:** Every draft. This is a revision rule, not a drafting rule — write the warm-up freely, then remove it.
**Apply it in prose:** After finishing a draft, delete the first paragraph and reread. If nothing essential is lost (it almost never is), keep the cut; fold any truly necessary fact into a later sentence. For short posts, apply it at the sentence level: cut the first sentence.

## Start inside the action, skip the lead-up
Open at the moment something is happening — mid-scene, mid-crisis, mid-decision — rather than with the background that led there. Backstory answers questions the reader hasn't asked yet; action creates the questions. A reader dropped into motion orients themselves eagerly, and that orienting effort is engagement.
**When to use:** Any narrative opening: case studies, founder stories, newsletters, talk openers turned into posts.
**Apply it in prose:** Find the single most tense or vivid moment in your material and open there, even if it's chronologically the middle: "The CFO slid the laptop shut and said, 'We're done funding this.'" Backfill context later, in fragments, only as the reader needs it.

## Set the scene in one plain line
Establish time and place with a single unadorned sentence — his model is the bare "The year is 1963." One flat, declarative scene-setter does the work of a paragraph of description because the reader's imagination fills in the era, the room, the mood — and what readers imagine themselves is more vivid than anything you could write.
**When to use:** Openings and scene transitions in any narrative passage.
**Apply it in prose:** Replace descriptive wind-ups with one short factual line: "It's 2 a.m. in a hospital parking lot." / "March 2020, week one of lockdown." Then move immediately to action. Trust the reader; add a second detail only if the scene would be misread without it.

## Withhold the why — unresolved curiosity is engagement
Don't explain up front where the story is going or why you're telling it. A reader thinking "where is this going?" is not confused — they're hooked; that open question is the tension that keeps them reading, and answering it early releases the tension prematurely.
**When to use:** Narrative sections of essays, talks, and posts. (Not for utility content like docs, where readers need orientation first.)
**Apply it in prose:** Cut signposting like "Let me tell you a story that illustrates the importance of trust." Just tell the story. Hold the connection between story and point until the story has done its work — and tolerate the mild disorientation; it's doing the pulling.

## Lesson only after the story
Deliver the moral, takeaway, or business point only once the narrative is complete. Explaining first turns story into lecture: the reader knows what they're supposed to feel, so they audit the story instead of experiencing it. Story first lets them arrive at the feeling honestly; the stated lesson then confirms rather than instructs.
**When to use:** Any story-plus-takeaway format — the backbone of LinkedIn storytelling, keynotes, and parable-style newsletters.
**Apply it in prose:** Structure strictly as story → white space → lesson. The lesson should be short — a few sentences at most — because the story carried the weight. If the lesson needs a paragraph of explaining, the story didn't do its job; fix the story, not the explanation.

## Specific emotional stakes
Render stakes in precise, countable, human detail: "47 email threads," "the 3 a.m. call," "her fourth resignation letter that quarter" — never "a lot of confusion" or "significant challenges." Specificity is credibility and imagery at once: exact numbers feel witnessed rather than invented, and concrete images are what emotions attach to.
**When to use:** Whenever a draft describes a problem, cost, or turning point in general terms.
**Apply it in prose:** Sweep the draft for vague magnitude words ("many," "huge," "constant," "significant") and replace each with a specific, truthful detail — a number, a time of day, a named object. If you don't know the specific, ask the user for it; don't fabricate precision about real events.

## Start with why
Lead with the belief or purpose behind the work — why it exists — before what it is or how it works, because as his golden circle argument goes, people don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it. Purpose framed first gives readers something to belong to; features framed first give them something to compare.
**When to use:** Brand narratives, about pages, manifestos, recruiting content, launch posts for mission-driven products.
**Apply it in prose:** Write the cause as a belief statement before any product sentence: "We believe no one should need a lawyer to understand their own lease." Let what/how follow as evidence of the belief. Test: could a competitor paste your opening over their product? If yes, the why isn't specific enough.

## Before / after
**Flat:** In today's fast-paced business environment, communication is more important than ever. I want to share a story about a project that taught me the value of clear communication between teams.
**With Sinek's technique:** The year is 2021. It's 11 p.m., and I'm reading email thread number 47 about a feature we shipped six weeks ago — that, I'm about to learn, two teams built twice. Nobody was lazy. Nobody was dumb. Every person in those threads was doing their job well. That's what scared me. It took three more duplicated projects before I understood what was actually broken — and it wasn't communication.

## Best for
LinkedIn storytelling posts, keynote-style essays, brand manifestos and about pages, founder narratives, newsletters built on parable structure, mission-driven launch copy.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Did you delete the original first paragraph (or first sentence) after drafting?
- [ ] Does the piece open inside an action or moment rather than with background?
- [ ] Is the scene set in one plain line the reader's imagination can complete?
- [ ] Is the point withheld until the story is finished — no "let me tell you why this matters" upfront?
- [ ] Are all stakes rendered in specific, countable detail instead of vague magnitude words?
- [ ] For brand/mission pieces: does a belief statement come before any what/how — and is it one a competitor couldn't copy-paste?
