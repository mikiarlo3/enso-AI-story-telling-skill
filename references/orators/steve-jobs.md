# Steve Jobs — Product Narrative & The Reveal

Apple co-founder whose keynotes turned product launches into cultural events. His philosophy: a product announcement is a drama with a villain, a hero, and one unforgettable line — everything else gets cut.

## Three-act keynote structure
Structure the piece as a drama: Act 1 establishes the world and its problem, Act 2 builds tension by showing why existing solutions fail, Act 3 delivers the resolution — the product, idea, or answer. Withholding the resolution until the reader feels the problem makes the solution feel inevitable rather than salesy.
**When to use:** Product launches, feature announcements, "introducing X" posts, pitch narratives.
**Apply it in prose:** Outline three headings before drafting: "the world today" (problem, no product mention), "why nothing works" (survey failed alternatives honestly), "here's the answer" (your thing, arriving as relief). Resist naming your product in Acts 1–2.

## Villain and hero framing
Personify the problem as a villain — complexity, lock-in, wasted time, an industry norm — and cast the product as the hero that defeats it. Conflict is the engine of story; a product with no antagonist is just a spec sheet, but a product fighting something gives readers a side to root for.
**When to use:** Competitive markets, category-creation copy, any launch where "why does this exist?" needs answering.
**Apply it in prose:** Name the villain explicitly and early ("the real enemy here is the settings menu"). Make it a condition or behavior, not usually a named competitor — punching at a status quo ages better than punching at a brand. Then show the hero winning specific fights against it.

## The withheld reveal
Save the best detail for last and signal that something is still coming. Jobs's "one more thing" worked because the audience believed the show was over — the surprise landed on a closed loop. In prose, a promised-but-delayed payoff keeps readers scrolling past the point they'd normally stop.
**When to use:** Announcements with a genuinely surprising extra (a price, a free tier, a second product). Never fake it — a weak reveal spends trust.
**Apply it in prose:** Structure the piece so it feels complete, then add a short final section that reframes everything ("And one more thing: it's free."). Alternatively, tease early — "there's a detail at the end that changes the math" — and pay it off in the last two lines.

## Demo as story
Don't describe capabilities; walk the reader through one concrete moment of use, in sequence, with a small "wow" beat. A demo is narrative proof: the reader experiences the product working instead of being told it works, which converts skepticism into vicarious ownership.
**When to use:** Feature explainers, onboarding emails, landing-page body copy.
**Apply it in prose:** Write a present-tense walkthrough of one specific task by one specific user: "You paste the link. Two seconds later, the summary is on your screen. You didn't open a single tab." Keep it to one scenario done vividly rather than five done vaguely.

## Ruthless message simplicity — one headline per thing
Give every product, feature, or idea exactly one headline: a short, concrete, sensory line that translates a spec into a life benefit — the pattern behind "1,000 songs in your pocket." One line survives retelling; a paragraph of benefits does not, and word-of-mouth is the real distribution channel.
**When to use:** Always — write the headline before the piece; if you can't, the positioning isn't done.
**Apply it in prose:** Draft ten candidate one-liners that pair a concrete number or image with a personal outcome. Pick one. Use it as the title or first line, repeat it once near the end, and delete every secondary message that competes with it.

## Rule of three in structure
Organize the piece's skeleton in threes: three problems, three pillars, three reasons. Three is the smallest number that feels like a pattern and the largest an audience reliably remembers, so it reads as complete without overwhelming.
**When to use:** Announcements covering multiple features, strategy posts, recap content.
**Apply it in prose:** If you have five points, cut or merge to three and say so ("It comes down to three things"). Give the three parallel grammatical form and parallel-length sections. If one point matters most, put it third.

## Before / after
**Flat:** We're excited to announce version 4.0 of our analytics platform, which includes improved dashboards, faster queries, and a new API.
**With Jobs's technique:** Every Monday, you lose your morning to the same ritual: exporting CSVs, fixing broken charts, apologizing for stale numbers. The tools were built for analysts, not for you. Today that ends. Meet 4.0 — your whole business, live, in one screen. Three things make it different: it's fast, it's yours to shape, and it talks to everything you already use. And one more thing: the dashboard your team looks at every day is now free.

## Best for
Product launches and announcements, landing pages, feature explainers, pitch decks turned into prose, keynote-style blog posts, positioning and category-creation copy.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is there a named villain the product visibly defeats?
- [ ] Does the product stay unnamed until the problem is fully felt?
- [ ] Can the whole piece be compressed into one concrete, retellable headline — and does that line appear first?
- [ ] Is there a walkthrough of one real moment of use instead of a capability list?
- [ ] Is the structure built on threes rather than an unranked feature dump?
- [ ] If there's a final reveal, is it genuinely the strongest card in the piece?
