# Kindra Hall — Strategic Business Storytelling

Keynote speaker and author of Stories That Stick, who turned business storytelling from a vague virtue into an operating system with named story types and a repeatable structure. Her philosophy in one sentence: the right story, told at the right time, closes the gaps in business — and most companies are sitting on those stories without telling them.

## Pick the story type by the business goal
There are four essential business stories, and choosing by goal is the whole game: the Value story (dramatize what the product does for a real person) drives sales and marketing; the Founder story (the origin) builds trust with investors, customers, and hires; the Purpose story (why we exist) aligns teams and culture; the Customer story (a client's journey in their own experience) delivers proof. It works because each gap — belief, trust, alignment, credibility — is closed by a different narrative.
**When to use:** at the planning stage of any piece, before a word is drafted.
**Apply it in prose:** name the gap first ("readers don't believe the product matters to them" → Value story; "readers don't trust us yet" → Founder story; "team is drifting" → Purpose story; "claims need receipts" → Customer story). If a draft is wandering, it's usually telling the wrong type for its goal — a Founder story on a product page, a Value story in a culture memo.

## Normal → Explosion → New Normal
Every working story has three beats: the Normal (life as it was), the Explosion (the moment something changed — a product, a decision, a discovery), and the New Normal (life after). Business content habitually starts at the Explosion and skips the Normal — which is exactly backward, because the Normal is where the audience recognizes themselves, and without that recognition the change lands on no one.
**When to use:** structuring any narrative passage — case studies, founder pages, launch posts, speeches.
**Apply it in prose:** spend real time in the Normal — several sentences of the character's ordinary life, frustrations, workarounds, and what they'd resigned themselves to — before anything changes. Then let the Explosion be a specific moment, not a gradual trend. Close with a New Normal drawn in the same everyday terms as the Normal, so the contrast does the selling.

## Identifiable character
A story needs one person the audience can picture and follow — not "our customers," not "the company," but a single named human — because empathy attaches to individuals, and a story without a character is a report.
**When to use:** any time a draft speaks in aggregates, personas, or corporate "we."
**Apply it in prose:** choose one real person (customer, founder, employee) and keep the camera on them for the whole story. Give them two or three humanizing particulars — role, habit, a line they actually said. If the character is a composite or anonymized, keep them singular and concrete anyway; "a nurse on her third night shift" beats "healthcare workers."

## Authentic emotion
The story must include what the character felt — worry, embarrassment, relief, pride — stated honestly and proportionately, because emotion is the carrier signal of memory: audiences forget the feature list and remember the feeling.
**When to use:** everywhere business writing goes bloodless — case studies, announcements, internal comms.
**Apply it in prose:** at each beat, add one line of interior weather: what the character felt in the Normal ("she'd stopped mentioning the backlog in standups; it was too embarrassing") and in the New Normal ("the first quiet Friday felt like a trick"). Show it through behavior and small admissions rather than labels; "she checked her phone at 2 a.m. anyway" outworks "she was anxious."

## The significant moment
Compress the change into one specific scene — a point in space and time the audience can stand inside — because moments are rememberable and reproducible in the mind, while summaries ("over the next six months, things improved") evaporate on contact.
**When to use:** the Explosion beat; testimonials; turning points in any narrative.
**Apply it in prose:** find the scene: the demo where the room went silent, the Tuesday the error count read zero, the phone call in the parking lot. Write it with scene mechanics — where they were, what was said, what happened next — in two to four sentences. If the draft's turning point is a date range, keep digging until it's a day.

## Specific details
A few precise, well-chosen details — the brand of coffee, the 47 open browser tabs, the spreadsheet named "FINAL_v9" — make a story feel true and pull the audience into co-creating it, because details they recognize from their own life convert listeners into participants. Vagueness, by contrast, signals invention.
**When to use:** every story beat; especially the Normal, where recognition is the goal.
**Apply it in prose:** replace each generic noun with the particular one the character would name ("software" → "the 11-tab spreadsheet she rebuilt every Monday"). Choose details the target audience knows from the inside — that's what makes them lean in — and cap them: three sharp details beat ten decorative ones. Any detail that could appear in a competitor's story unchanged isn't specific enough.

## Before / after
**Flat:** XYZ Accounting helps small businesses save time on bookkeeping. Our clients love how easy our software is to use. Try it free for 30 days.
**With Hall's technique:** Every Sunday night, Marta closed her bakery, poured the day's last coffee, and spread receipts across the counter where the croissants had been. Three hours, most weeks — she called it "my second shift," and she'd stopped expecting it to change. Then one Sunday in March she photographed the first receipt with our app, mostly to prove it wouldn't work. It took four seconds. She sat there and laughed out loud in the empty shop. Sundays are hers again; the receipts never made it back to the counter. Try the four-second version of your second shift — free for 30 days.

## Best for
Case studies and testimonials, founder and about pages, keynote and speech writing, culture and internal communications, brand campaigns, fundraising narratives — any content where a claim needs to be felt rather than merely stated.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is the story type (Value, Founder, Purpose, Customer) deliberately matched to the piece's goal?
- [ ] Does the story spend real time in the Normal before anything changes?
- [ ] Is there one identifiable character the camera stays on throughout?
- [ ] Is at least one honest emotion shown through behavior, not just labeled?
- [ ] Is the change compressed into a single specific scene rather than a summary?
- [ ] Would the target audience recognize the details as drawn from their own life?
