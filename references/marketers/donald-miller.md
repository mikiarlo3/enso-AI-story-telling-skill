# Donald Miller — StoryBrand Clarity

Author of Building a StoryBrand and creator of the SB7 framework, which maps classic hero's-journey structure onto marketing messages. His philosophy in one sentence: the customer is the hero of the story and the brand is the guide — and clarity always beats cleverness, because "if you confuse, you lose."

## Cast the customer as hero, the brand as guide
The reader is Luke Skywalker; the brand is Yoda. This works because readers scan every message for their own survival and success — a brand that plays hero competes with the reader for the starring role, while a guide earns trust by serving the hero's story.
**When to use:** all brand and marketing prose — about pages, home pages, sales emails, case studies.
**Apply it in prose:** count the sentences whose grammatical subject is "we/our" versus "you/your"; the reader should star in most of them. Rewrite achievements as service: not "we've spent ten years perfecting this," but "you get ten years of testing behind every decision you make with it." The guide shows two things only: empathy ("we know how frustrating this is") and competence ("we've guided 400 teams through it").

## Name the problem in three layers
Every purchase solves an external problem (the visible thing), but people buy to resolve the internal problem (how it makes them feel) and are loyal to the philosophical problem (why it's simply wrong). Copy that addresses only the external layer feels like a spec sheet; the internal layer is where motivation lives.
**When to use:** problem sections, headlines, empathy-building openings.
**Apply it in prose:** write all three layers before drafting — external ("invoices take four hours a week"), internal ("you feel like an accountant instead of a founder"), philosophical ("paperwork shouldn't be the price of doing work you love"). Lead with the internal or philosophical layer; use the external one as evidence.

## Give them a plan
A named, three-step plan converts intent into action because ambiguity creates risk, and people don't step into fog — stones across the creek get crossed, open water doesn't.
**When to use:** landing pages, onboarding copy, proposals, any conversion moment.
**Apply it in prose:** compress the path to "1. [simple verb], 2. [simple verb], 3. [outcome]" — "1. Book a call. 2. Get your custom plan. 3. Launch in 30 days." Three steps, even if reality has twelve; the plan's job is to shrink perceived risk, not document the process.

## One clear call to action
A confused mind does nothing, so every piece gets exactly one direct CTA — repeated, visually and verbally identical — because each additional option divides the reader's momentum.
**When to use:** every piece of marketing prose, ending of every post or email.
**Apply it in prose:** pick one direct CTA ("Schedule a demo") and state it with the same wording each time it appears. If you need a softer path, add one transitional CTA (a free guide, a checklist) for readers not ready to buy — but never a menu. Cut "learn more," "explore," and other verbs that ask for nothing.

## Show the stakes: success and failure
Stories run on stakes. Paint what life looks like if the reader succeeds, and honestly name what continues or worsens if they don't — because a story where nothing can be lost is a story nobody follows.
**When to use:** closing sections, before the CTA, nurture emails.
**Apply it in prose:** dedicate a few lines to the success picture in sensory, day-in-the-life terms ("Monday morning, the report is already in your inbox"). Then one or two restrained lines of failure stakes ("or keep rebuilding the same spreadsheet every quarter"). Failure should be a pinch of salt, not the meal — dwelling on doom reads as manipulation.

## Clarity beats cleverness
Readers burn calories to process messages, and they won't. A clever headline that requires decoding loses to a plain one that survives the grunt test: could a stranger glance for five seconds and say what you offer, how it improves their life, and how to get it?
**When to use:** headlines, taglines, first paragraphs, subject lines.
**Apply it in prose:** run the grunt test on the opening. Replace abstractions and wordplay with survival-relevant plain language: "We disrupt paradigms" fails; "We help freelancers get paid on time" passes. If a line makes you feel witty, check whether it makes the reader feel informed.

## Before / after
**Flat:** At Meridian Analytics, we leverage cutting-edge AI to deliver best-in-class business intelligence solutions. Our award-winning platform empowers data-driven transformation.
**With Miller's technique:** You shouldn't need a data science degree to know how your business is doing. Most owners drown in dashboards and still guess. Meridian gives you one plain-English report each Monday. 1. Connect your tools. 2. Pick your questions. 3. Read one page a week. Schedule a demo — and stop guessing.

## Best for
Website copy, landing pages, sales emails, product launches, pitch narratives, nonprofit appeals — any prose whose job is a clear next action.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is the customer the grammatical subject of most sentences — hero, not audience?
- [ ] Does the brand show up only as a guide, expressing empathy and competence?
- [ ] Are all three problem layers present, with the internal one carrying the emotional weight?
- [ ] Is there a simple named plan of about three steps?
- [ ] Is there exactly one direct call to action, repeated verbatim?
- [ ] Would a stranger pass the five-second grunt test on the opening?
