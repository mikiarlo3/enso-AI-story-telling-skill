# Nancy Duarte — persuasive structure and the sparkline

Nancy Duarte is a presentation designer and communication theorist, author of *Resonate*, who reverse-engineered the shape of history's great speeches. Her philosophy in one sentence: persuasion is structural — you move people by repeatedly contrasting the world as it is with the world as it could be until they want to cross the gap themselves.

## The sparkline
Alternate repeatedly between "what is" (the flawed present) and "what could be" (the better future), because each swing re-opens the gap between reality and possibility, and that unresolved tension is what pulls the audience forward. One contrast persuades once; repeated contrast builds momentum.
**When to use:** The overall skeleton of any persuasive piece — pitch, manifesto, launch post, keynote.
**Apply it in prose:** Map your draft's paragraphs and label each "what is" or "what could be." If three consecutive paragraphs carry the same label, restructure so the piece oscillates. End every "what is" passage with an implicit itch the next passage scratches.

## Establish the status quo, then reveal the gap fast
Open by describing the audience's current world accurately enough that they nod along — then puncture it with the first "what could be," the call to adventure, because agreement earns you the right to disrupt, and an early gap tells readers exactly what journey they're on.
**When to use:** Openings of persuasive prose; the first 10–15% of the piece.
**Apply it in prose:** Paragraph one: their reality, in their words, no judgment. Paragraph two: the turn — "It doesn't have to work this way." Don't delay the turn past the first few paragraphs; a long status quo reads as endorsement of it.

## The STAR moment
Plant one Something They'll Always Remember — a shocking statistic, a vivid mini-story, a dramatized demonstration, or a repeatable sound bite — because audiences retain a single peak, and if you don't choose it deliberately, they'll remember something incidental or nothing at all.
**When to use:** Once per piece, positioned near your most important claim.
**Apply it in prose:** Pick the one claim that must survive sharing and forwarding, then escalate its expression: convert the stat into a human-scale image, compress the argument into a quotable line, or drop a two-sentence story that embodies it. One STAR moment only — two competing peaks flatten each other.

## The new-bliss ending
Close by painting the world after the audience has adopted the idea — what their week, their team, their metrics look like on the other side — because people act on futures they can picture themselves inside, not on summaries of your argument.
**When to use:** The final paragraph of anything persuasive.
**Apply it in prose:** Ban recap endings ("In conclusion, we've seen..."). Instead, write the reader into the future, second person, concrete detail: "Six months from now, your Monday standup takes eight minutes." End on the reward, not the request.

## Audience as hero, presenter as mentor
Cast the reader as Luke and yourself as Yoda: you supply the tool, the map, or the magic gift, but the reader takes the journey and wins the victory — because people resist being told a story about how great you are, and lean into a story about who they could become.
**When to use:** Every piece with a brand or author voice; especially marketing copy and thought leadership.
**Apply it in prose:** Audit pronouns. If "we/I/our product" dominates, rewrite so "you" drives the verbs and the brand appears as the helper. "You'll ship in half the time" beats "Our platform accelerates shipping."

## Call to action as the second turning point
Place the explicit ask just before the close — after the final "what is / what could be" contrast and before the new-bliss picture — because an ask made at peak tension, then immediately rewarded with the vision of bliss, feels like a doorway rather than a demand.
**When to use:** Any piece that ends in a next step: sign up, reply, adopt, vote, buy.
**Apply it in prose:** Sequence the last three beats deliberately: (1) sharpest contrast, (2) one specific, doable action, (3) new bliss. Make the action singular — a list of three CTAs is zero CTAs.

## Paragraph-level sparkline template for prose
Use this as a default skeleton for a persuasive post or email:
1. **Para 1 — What is:** the reader's current reality, described so precisely they feel seen.
2. **Para 2 — What could be:** the turn; the gap revealed; the call to adventure.
3. **Para 3 — What is:** the obstacle or cost of staying put, made concrete.
4. **Para 4 — What could be:** proof it's crossable — evidence, mini-story, or the STAR moment.
5. **Para 5 — What is → ask:** the last look at the flawed present, then one clear call to action.
6. **Para 6 — New bliss:** the reader's life after adopting the idea; end on this image.
Lengthen by adding more is/could-be pairs between beats 2 and 5; never end on "what is."

## Before / after
**Flat:** Our new scheduling tool has many features that help teams save time. Sign up today to learn more about how we can improve your workflow.
**With Duarte's technique:** Right now, your team spends Monday morning trading nineteen messages to book one meeting. It doesn't have to. Imagine a Monday where the calendar sorts itself before your coffee cools. Today, every reschedule steals twenty minutes you never log. Teams who switched last quarter got back a full workday a month — one manager called it "found money." Start a free trial this week; it takes four minutes. Then next Monday, watch your standup start on time — and end early.

## Best for
Persuasive essays, product launches, sales pages, fundraising and pitch narratives, keynote speeches, manifesto-style blog posts, change-management communications — anywhere the goal is adoption of an idea or action.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Does the piece alternate between "what is" and "what could be" at least twice?
- [ ] Does the first "what could be" (the gap) appear within the opening 15%?
- [ ] Is there exactly one deliberate STAR moment?
- [ ] Is the reader the hero (audit: does "you" outnumber "we/I")?
- [ ] Is there one specific call to action just before the close?
- [ ] Does the piece end on new bliss — a picture of the reader's better future — not a recap?
