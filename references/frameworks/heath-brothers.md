# Chip & Dan Heath — sticky ideas without a story arc

Chip and Dan Heath are the brother duo behind *Made to Stick*, a study of why some ideas survive and spread while better-funded ones die. Their philosophy in one sentence: stickiness is not talent but a checklist — Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories (SUCCESs) — applicable to a six-word headline as much as a six-page essay.

**Use this file as the fallback when there is no room for a narrative arc:** headlines, taglines, subject lines, short LinkedIn hooks, ad copy, slide titles, product one-liners. The other frameworks need runway; SUCCESs works at any length.

## Simple: find the core
Strip the idea to its single most important claim — a "commander's intent" sentence that survives compression and still guides action when every supporting detail is forgotten. It works because readers can act on one prioritized idea, but paralyze on three equally-weighted ones.
**When to use:** Before writing anything; mandatory for headlines and taglines.
**Apply it in prose:** Write the one sentence a reader should repeat to a colleague tomorrow. If your draft buries it, promote it to the headline or first line. When forced to choose between two good points, cut one — a Simple idea is compact *and* core, not merely short.

## Unexpected: break a schema, then open a curiosity gap
First violate the reader's existing pattern (surprise gets attention), then pose a question their current knowledge can't answer (curiosity holds attention until you close the gap). It works because the brain flags prediction errors as must-resolve, and an open knowledge gap is mildly painful to leave open.
**When to use:** Hooks, subject lines, opening sentences.
**Apply it in prose:** State the counterintuitive fact plainly ("The safest seat on a plane is the one nobody wants"), then delay the resolution just long enough to earn the click or the next paragraph. Always close the gap you open — unresolved bait destroys trust.

## Concrete: sensory, specific language
Replace abstractions with things a reader can see, touch, or picture — this is the antidote to the Curse of Knowledge, the expert's inability to remember what not-knowing felt like, which pushes writing toward jargon and generality. Concrete language works because memory is built from sensory hooks, not category labels.
**When to use:** Every line; especially wherever your draft says "solutions," "value," "innovative," "streamline."
**Apply it in prose:** Run the imagination test: can the reader picture it? "Optimize collaboration" fails; "stop losing decisions in a 400-message thread" passes. Convert each abstraction into one named object, action, or number at human scale.

## Credible: humanized statistics and testable claims
Make the reader able to verify the claim themselves — through a statistic scaled to their lived experience, a vivid authority, or a "try it and see" challenge — because borrowed trust ("experts agree") is weaker than trust the reader generates internally.
**When to use:** Any claim a skeptic would challenge; B2B copy, landing pages, data-heavy posts.
**Apply it in prose:** Humanize every number: not "13,000 hours saved" but "every engineer gets their Friday afternoons back." Where possible, issue a testable claim the reader can check in their own world: "Open your calendar — count the meetings with no agenda."

## Emotional: make them feel for one, not for millions
Attach the idea to one identified individual rather than aggregate numbers, because people feel for people and go analytical (and stingy, and unmoved) when shown statistics — the identifiable-victim effect the Heaths illustrate with Mother Teresa's line, "If I look at the mass I will never act; if I look at the one, I will."
**When to use:** Fundraising, mission statements, any copy meant to move rather than inform.
**Apply it in prose:** Lead with one named person and their particular stake; let the statistic follow as scale, never as the hook. Also appeal to identity, not just gain: "people like you don't ship sloppy work" outpulls "save 10%."

## Stories: a flight simulator for the reader's brain
Even in two sentences, narrating an event lets readers mentally rehearse the action — a story is a flight simulator, building readiness the way abstract advice never can — because simulating an experience recruits much of the same mental machinery as having it.
**When to use:** When you have three sentences of room but not thirty; social proof, onboarding copy, examples.
**Apply it in prose:** Compress to micro-story: a person, a moment, an outcome. "A support rep in Ohio tried the macro on her worst ticket queue. She cleared it by lunch." No arc required — just a specific someone doing the thing you want the reader to imagine doing.

## Before / after
**Flat:** Our cybersecurity platform leverages advanced AI to significantly reduce organizational risk. Thousands of companies trust our innovative solutions.
**With the Heaths' technique:** One stolen password ended a 40-year-old company. (It was taped under a keyboard.) Ninety seconds of setup makes yours the firm that survives the audit everyone else fails — run our free scan and count your exposed logins right now. Last month, an office manager in Tulsa found eleven. She fixed them before her second coffee.

## Best for
Headlines, taglines, subject lines, LinkedIn hooks, ad copy, slide titles, product descriptions, executive summaries, calls to action — and as a final polish pass over any longer piece written with the other frameworks.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Can the core idea survive as one sentence a reader could repeat tomorrow?
- [ ] Does the opening break an expectation and open a curiosity gap you later close?
- [ ] Could the reader picture every key line (no un-visualizable abstractions left)?
- [ ] Is every statistic translated to human scale or made testable?
- [ ] Does emotion route through one identified person before any aggregate number?
- [ ] Is at least one claim carried by a micro-story rather than an assertion?
