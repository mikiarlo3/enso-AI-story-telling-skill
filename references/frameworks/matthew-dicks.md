# Matthew Dicks — five-second moments and everyday stakes

Matthew Dicks is a record-setting Moth StorySLAM champion and the author of *Storyworthy*, a craft manual for true personal stories. His philosophy in one sentence: every story exists to deliver a single five-second moment of change, and everything else on the page is engineered to make that moment land.

## The five-second moment
Every story is about one instant in which a person changes — a realization, a reversal, a shift in belief. Identify that instant before drafting anything, because a piece with no change is an anecdote, not a story, and readers feel the difference as pointlessness.
**When to use:** Before outlining any narrative content — a founder story, a customer story, a personal LinkedIn post.
**Apply it in prose:** Write one sentence: "This is the moment X stopped believing A and started believing B." If you cannot write it, you do not have a story yet — find the change or cut the narrative frame.

## Start at the opposite of your ending
The opening should embody the reverse of the transformation the ending delivers, because contrast is what makes change visible; if the hero ends confident, open on doubt. This turns the whole piece into a measurable arc.
**When to use:** Structuring any before/after narrative — case studies, launch posts, personal essays.
**Apply it in prose:** Locate your five-second moment, name the end state in one word (trust, clarity, courage), then draft an opening scene or sentence that dramatizes its opposite. Check that the first and last paragraphs could not be swapped.

## Homework for Life
Capture one storyworthy moment from each day in a single sentence — small moments beat big ones, because readers connect to the recognizable (a tense pause in a meeting) far more than the extraordinary (winning an award). The habit also trains you to see stories everywhere.
**When to use:** Building a content pipeline; escaping "I have nothing to write about."
**Apply it in prose:** When drafting for a client or brand, ask for (or mine interviews for) small daily moments, not milestones. Prefer "the intern who refreshed the dashboard forty times" over "we hit 1M users."

## The elephant
State the thing the story is about — the need, the problem, the mystery, the want — in the first few lines, because readers who don't know what to care about stop caring. The elephant can later turn out to be something else, but one must be visible immediately.
**When to use:** Openings of any prose longer than a paragraph, especially newsletters and blog posts.
**Apply it in prose:** By the end of paragraph one, the reader should be able to answer "what question is this piece going to resolve?" If they can't, add an explicit stake: a problem, a deadline, a doubt.

## The backpack
Load the reader with the protagonist's hopes and plan *before* the event happens, so the reader carries the stakes into the scene and experiences the outcome as their own win or loss rather than as reported news.
**When to use:** Any scene with an outcome — a pitch, a launch, a difficult conversation, a product test.
**Apply it in prose:** Before narrating what happened, spend two or three sentences on what the protagonist expected, planned, and feared. "We had rehearsed the demo eleven times. If it froze again, the deal was dead." Then let the event unfold.

## Breadcrumbs
Hint at what's coming while revealing only enough to keep the reader guessing, because an active prediction loop is what makes prose feel unputdownable. A breadcrumb is a promise, not a spoiler.
**When to use:** Mid-piece, wherever attention typically sags — the second third of a long post or speech.
**Apply it in prose:** Drop a concrete but incomplete detail early ("I kept the voicemail; I still have it") and pay it off later. Never explain a breadcrumb when you drop it.

## The hourglass
Slow the prose down at the climax — spend your words where the change happens — because pacing signals importance; racing through the five-second moment tells readers it didn't matter. Compress everything else instead.
**When to use:** The paragraph containing your five-second moment.
**Apply it in prose:** At the turning point, shift to short sentences, present-tense sensory detail, and moment-by-moment action. If your climax currently occupies one sentence and your setup occupies ten, invert the ratio of attention.

## The crystal ball
Voice a prediction that may turn out wrong — the protagonist's guess about what happens next — because a stated expectation creates dread or hope the reader must resolve by reading on.
**When to use:** Just before an uncertain outcome; great for suspense in case studies and founder stories.
**Apply it in prose:** Insert a line of anticipated future: "I was sure the board would say no." Whether the prediction lands or shatters, the reader was leaning forward for the answer.

## Before / after
**Flat:** Our migration to the new platform was challenging, but after months of work, the team succeeded and performance improved by 40%.
**With Dicks's technique:** In March, I told the team the migration would take three weekends. (I still have the Slack message.) By weekend nine, half of them had stopped believing me — and honestly, so had I. Then, at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday, Priya ran the benchmark one more time. She didn't say anything. She just turned her laptop around. Forty percent faster. That was the moment I stopped thinking of deadlines as promises and started thinking of them as guesses.

## Best for
Personal essays, founder and origin stories, LinkedIn narrative posts, customer stories and case studies, speeches and talks, "lessons learned" newsletters — anywhere a human being changes on the page.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Can you state the five-second moment of change in one sentence?
- [ ] Does the opening show the opposite of the end state?
- [ ] Is the elephant (what to care about) visible in the first paragraph?
- [ ] Did you pack the backpack — hopes and plan — before the key event?
- [ ] Does the prose slow down at the moment of change, not before it?
- [ ] Is the central moment small and recognizable rather than grand and generic?
