# Eugene Schwartz — Market Awareness & Desire Channeling

Author of *Breakthrough Advertising*, the most studied theory book in copywriting. His philosophy: copy doesn't create desire — it takes the desire already burning in millions of people and focuses it onto one particular solution.

## Match the opening to the reader's awareness stage
Schwartz mapped five stages of market awareness: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, and most-aware. The right opening depends entirely on which stage the reader is in — leading with your product to someone who doesn't yet know they have a problem wastes the message, while re-explaining the problem to someone ready to buy bores them away.
**When to use:** Before writing the first line of anything persuasive.
**Apply it in prose:** Diagnose the reader first. Unaware: open with a story or identity they recognize, and let the problem surface. Problem-aware: open by naming their pain more precisely than they can. Solution-aware: open with the mechanism and proof it works. Product-aware: open with what makes yours different. Most-aware: open with the offer, the price, the deadline. Never write one opening for all five.

## Channel desire; never try to create it
Mass desire — for health, status, security, love, ease — already exists at a scale no writer can manufacture. Copy works when it takes that existing current and directs it toward one product, because you are riding a river, not digging one.
**When to use:** Positioning any product, idea, or call to action.
**Apply it in prose:** Identify the pre-existing desire your subject actually connects to (not the desire you wish it connected to). Write the piece so the product appears as the newest, best vehicle for a want the reader walked in with. If the piece is trying to convince readers to want something new, reframe until it serves a want they already have.

## Assemble copy from research, not imagination
Schwartz described his job as connection, not creation: the winning lines already exist in customer language, product facts, and competitor claims, and the writer's work is gathering and arranging them. Invented copy sounds like a writer; assembled copy sounds like the reader's own mind.
**When to use:** Any project where you can access reviews, interviews, support tickets, forums, or source material.
**Apply it in prose:** Before drafting, collect verbatim customer phrasing — how real people describe the problem and the dream outcome. Build headlines and key lines by sharpening those phrases rather than composing from scratch. When a sentence feels flat, go back to the raw material instead of straining for cleverness.

## Intensify desire by dimensionalizing the outcome
A claim states a benefit; dimensionalizing makes the reader live it — the specific morning, the specific moment, the specific sensation of the problem gone. Desire intensifies with vividness because the mind responds to experiences it can simulate, not abstractions it merely parses.
**When to use:** The middle of persuasive pieces, after the hook and before the ask.
**Apply it in prose:** Take each core benefit and write the scene of it happening: where the reader is, what they see, what someone says to them, what they no longer have to do. Replace "save time" with the Tuesday afternoon they get back. Stack two or three such scenes to build the wanting before you make the offer.

## Gradualize bold claims
A claim bigger than the reader's current belief snaps disbelief shut. Schwartz built acceptance step by step — starting from what the reader already accepts and moving one credible increment at a time — because each small agreement widens what the reader will believe next.
**When to use:** Any surprising claim, contrarian argument, or ambitious promise.
**Apply it in prose:** Open with a statement the reader already knows is true. Add the next fact that follows naturally, then the next, so the bold conclusion arrives as the obvious end of a chain rather than a leap. Never lead with the claim your reader is least prepared to believe.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "Our meditation app reduces stress. Download it now and feel calmer every day."
**With Schwartz's technique:** "You already know the 3 p.m. tightness in your chest isn't about today's meetings. What you may not know is why ten quiet minutes at lunch changes what your body does at 3 p.m. — and what that first tension-free afternoon feels like."

## Best for
Sales pages, launch sequences, ad copy, positioning documents, persuasive essays, email funnels where readers arrive at different awareness stages.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Did I diagnose the reader's awareness stage and match the opening to it?
- [ ] Does the piece channel a desire the reader already has, rather than trying to invent one?
- [ ] Are key lines built from real customer/audience language, not writer's-room phrasing?
- [ ] Is at least one benefit dimensionalized into a specific, livable scene?
- [ ] Do bold claims arrive by gradual steps from something the reader already believes?
- [ ] Would a most-aware reader and an unaware reader need different versions of this — and did I pick one deliberately?
