# Leo Burnett — Inherent Drama & Warm Believability

Founder of the Leo Burnett Company and father of the "Chicago school" of advertising — character-driven, warm, rooted in the product itself. His philosophy: the most powerful story is already inside the product; the writer's job is to find it and dramatize it, not to invent something cleverer.

## Find the inherent drama
Every product exists because someone made it for a reason and someone else needs it for a reason — and where those two reasons meet, there is drama worth telling. Burnett dug into the product itself for its story rather than importing borrowed excitement, because drama that grows from the thing rings true, while bolted-on cleverness rings hollow.
**When to use:** Positioning, brand storytelling, product pages, about pages.
**Apply it in prose:** Ask two questions before drafting: why did someone bother to make this, and what happens in a person's life at the moment they need it? Write the piece at the intersection — the late shift the tool saves, the worry the service ends. Never decorate a product with unrelated drama; excavate its own.

## Let warm archetypal characters carry the story
Burnett built enduring campaigns around characters embodying deep archetypes people already trust — the rugged individualist, the friendly giant, the cheerful helper — because a character smuggles meaning past skepticism that a stated claim would trigger. Logic argues; characters are simply believed.
**When to use:** Brand narratives, case studies, founder stories, any abstraction that needs a face.
**Apply it in prose:** Give the message a person: the specific customer, the specific maker, the specific user whose story embodies the point. Choose someone who fits a recognizable archetype (the craftsman, the protector, the underdog) and let their actions state the claim so you don't have to.

## Believability through warmth, not cleverness
Burnett distrusted advertising that showed off; he aimed for the shared, homely truths of ordinary life — what he called finding the corny things that are actually true. Warmth persuades because readers drop their defenses for writing that feels human and on their side, while cleverness keeps attention on the writer.
**When to use:** Brand voice, community content, sensitive topics, any audience tired of being marketed to.
**Apply it in prose:** When choosing between the witty line and the true-and-human line, take the human one. Use plain words, real domestic detail, and generosity toward the reader. Cut irony that positions you above the audience; write beside them instead.

## Reach for the stars in craft standards
Burnett's creed — "When you reach for the stars you may not quite get one, but you won't come up with a handful of mud either" — set impossible ambition as the working standard, because aiming merely at "good enough" reliably lands below it. The stretch itself raises the floor of the work.
**When to use:** Editing and revision; deciding when a draft is done.
**Apply it in prose:** After a competent draft, ask what the unforgettable version would look like — the image no competitor would dare, the honesty no one else risks — and revise toward it even if you land short. Never ship the first version that merely works.

## Earthy, sensory language over abstraction
Burnett collected striking vernacular phrases in a folder he called "Corny Language" and reached for words with dirt and weather in them, because concrete sensory words are felt in the body while abstractions are merely processed. The plainest physical word usually beats the impressive one.
**When to use:** Every draft's edit pass; headlines and key lines especially.
**Apply it in prose:** Keep your own swipe file of vivid plain phrases from real speech. In revision, swap abstract latinate words for physical ones — "leverage synergies" becomes "pull together"; "optimize" becomes "squeeze more out of." Test key lines by asking whether you can see, hear, or touch them.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "Our accounting software streamlines financial workflows for small businesses, saving time and reducing errors."
**With Burnett's technique:** "Maria locks the bakery at nine, and for eleven years the books kept her at the counter till midnight. Now the ovens are cold, the numbers are done, and she's home for her daughter's homework. That's what we actually make."

## Best for
Brand narratives, about pages, customer stories, mission-driven content, consumer marketing, speeches that need heart.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Did I find the drama inside the product itself — why it was made and when it's needed?
- [ ] Is there a specific human character carrying the message instead of an abstract claim?
- [ ] Did I choose the warm, true line over the merely clever one?
- [ ] Are the key lines built from concrete, sensory, plain-spoken words?
- [ ] Did I revise a working draft toward an unforgettable version before shipping?
- [ ] Does the writing stand beside the reader rather than above them?
