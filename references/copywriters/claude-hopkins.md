# Claude Hopkins — Reason-Why Copy & Scientific Advertising

Author of *Scientific Advertising* (1923) and the pioneer of measured, accountable copy. His philosophy: advertising is salesmanship in print — every line must do a salesman's work, and everything a salesman would be fired for, the writing must not do.

## Give a reason why
Every claim, offer, and discount needs a stated reason, because an unexplained claim reads as puffery while an explained one reads as information. "50% off" invites suspicion; "50% off because we overstocked for a cancelled contract" invites belief.
**When to use:** Any claim of quality, any discount, any request for action.
**Apply it in prose:** After each claim in your draft, add the word "because" and finish the sentence with a concrete fact. If you can't finish it truthfully, the claim is too weak to keep. Explain offers the way you'd justify them to a skeptical friend.

## Specifics over superlatives
Hopkins held that superlatives ("the best," "the finest") are discounted on sight, while a specific figure or process detail is accepted as fact — because people assume you wouldn't state a precise, checkable thing unless it were true. Precision itself is a credibility signal.
**When to use:** Every quality or performance claim.
**Apply it in prose:** Convert each superlative to a measurement, a count, or a named process step. "Trusted by many teams" becomes "used daily by 1,142 engineering teams." Where you can't measure, describe the concrete practice that produces the quality.

## The pre-emptive claim
Hopkins famously dramatized how Schlitz beer bottles were cleaned with live steam — a process every brewery used but none had described. Stating the fact everyone shares but nobody says wins ownership of it in the public mind, because to readers, the first teller appears to be the only one doing it.
**When to use:** Commodity markets and crowded categories where products genuinely resemble competitors.
**Apply it in prose:** Tour your subject's ordinary process for details outsiders have never heard: the sourcing step, the testing routine, the review ritual. Describe one such "boring" fact vividly and first. Don't claim exclusivity — the specificity does the differentiating on its own.

## Salesmanship in print
Judge writing by one standard: would a competent salesperson, face to face with one prospect, say this? Style, cleverness, and applause are irrelevant; the only question is whether each line moves one real person toward a decision — because ads (and posts) succeed one reader at a time.
**When to use:** Editing any persuasive draft.
**Apply it in prose:** Read the piece as a one-on-one conversation. Cut lines a salesperson would never say aloud — puns for their own sake, self-congratulation, vague mission statements. Keep only what informs, answers an objection, or advances the decision.

## Offer service, not hype
Hopkins found the strongest copy positions the seller as serving the reader — free samples, useful information, help without obligation — because people resist being sold but welcome being helped, and a genuine favor creates the standing to ask for the sale.
**When to use:** Content marketing, lead generation, newsletters, top-of-funnel pieces.
**Apply it in prose:** Make the piece itself useful even to someone who never buys: teach the method, share the checklist, answer the real question. Frame the call to action as the next helpful step ("see how yours compares") rather than a demand ("buy now").

## Test everything measurable
Hopkins built his career on coupon-tracked ads: opinions about copy are worthless next to counted responses, because the market routinely rejects what experts admire. Every question about wording is an empirical question if you set it up to be one.
**When to use:** Headlines, subject lines, CTAs, offers — anywhere a comparison can be run.
**Apply it in prose:** Write two or three genuinely different versions of the highest-leverage line (headline, subject, CTA), not cosmetic variants. Ship them against each other where possible; where not, at least state which measurable behavior the piece exists to move, and cut anything that doesn't serve it.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "Our coffee is the best you'll ever taste. Order a bag today!"
**With Hopkins's technique:** "We cup-test every lot three times and reject four in ten. The rejects get sold to other roasters. Try a bag of what passes — if it isn't the best coffee you've made at home, we'll refund it and you keep the bag."

## Best for
Product marketing, e-commerce copy, offer pages, commodity differentiation, discount announcements, any content whose success can be measured.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Does every claim and every offer carry a stated, truthful reason why?
- [ ] Have all superlatives been converted to specifics — numbers, counts, named processes?
- [ ] Did I state a concrete "boring" process fact competitors share but never mention?
- [ ] Would a good salesperson say each line, face to face, to one prospect?
- [ ] Does the piece serve the reader with real usefulness before it asks for anything?
- [ ] Is there a measurable action this piece drives, and versions of the key line to test?
