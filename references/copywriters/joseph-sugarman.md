# Joseph Sugarman — The Slippery Slide & Reading Momentum

Founder of JS&A and BluBlocker, author of *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*, master of long-form print ads that sold gadgets by mail. His philosophy: copy is an environment engineered so that once a reader starts, stopping feels harder than continuing.

## Build the slippery slide
Every element of the piece — headline, first line, captions, subheads — has exactly one purpose: to get the next element read. Persuasion happens only if reading continues, so momentum is the first product the writer builds, before any argument.
**When to use:** Any piece longer than a few lines, especially sales pages and essays.
**Apply it in prose:** Audit the draft transition by transition: does the end of each paragraph create a pull into the next? Restructure so questions raised early are answered later, and place your strongest curiosity points just before the spots where readers typically bail (after the intro, before the pitch).

## Make the first sentence short and loaded
Sugarman kept opening sentences tiny — a few words — because a short first sentence is frictionless to read, and reading it commits the reader to the second. Its content should spark curiosity, not deliver information; the information can wait until momentum exists.
**When to use:** Openings of posts, emails, sales pages, speeches.
**Apply it in prose:** Cut your opening sentence to under eight words. Make it a hook, an oddity, or a flat statement that demands explanation ("I almost deleted this feature." / "It failed twice."). Move any context it was carrying into paragraph two or three.

## Plant seeds of curiosity at the exits
Readers quit at natural stopping points — section ends, paragraph breaks before a scroll. Sugarman bridged those exits with short forward-leaning teasers so the reader's curiosity outweighs the urge to stop. Write your own bridges: "That's when it got strange." "Here's the part nobody mentions." "Which led to a bigger problem."
**When to use:** Ends of sections in long posts, before pricing or CTA sections, at newsletter fold points.
**Apply it in prose:** Find every place a reader could reasonably stop and add a one-line bridge that promises a specific payoff just ahead. Vary the phrasing so the device stays invisible; make sure each promised payoff actually arrives, or trust collapses.

## Sell the concept, not the product
Sugarman positioned each product as one big idea — a smoke detector became a vigilant "nose" that never sleeps — because people buy the story of what a thing means, and features only matter once the concept has been bought. The concept gives every feature a reason to be interesting.
**When to use:** Product marketing, launches, brand narratives, personal positioning.
**Apply it in prose:** Before drafting, write one sentence naming what the product *is really* — the metaphor, role, or transformation it represents. Open the piece with that concept, let every feature appear as evidence for it, and never open with a spec list.

## Harmonize before you ask
Sugarman opened by getting readers nodding — statements they already agree with, feelings they recognize — because a string of small agreements builds a rhythm of "yes" that carries into the ask, while an early disputed claim breaks rapport before it forms.
**When to use:** The first third of any persuasive piece, and any piece for a skeptical audience.
**Apply it in prose:** Start with two or three observations the reader will silently confirm from their own experience ("The demo always works. Production is where it breaks."). Only introduce your contested claim or offer after the reader has agreed with you several times.

## Answer objections where they occur
Sugarman raised the reader's objections himself, at the exact moment they'd naturally arise, because an unspoken doubt stops the slide as surely as a boring line — the reader leaves the text to argue with it. Naming the doubt keeps them reading and earns candor points.
**When to use:** Pricing reveals, bold claims, asks of any kind.
**Apply it in prose:** List the three doubts your reader will have and find the sentence in your draft where each one sparks. Voice it there in the reader's words ("You're thinking this only works for big teams") and answer it immediately, before moving on.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "Introducing our new standing desk, featuring a dual-motor lift system, memory presets, and a bamboo surface. Order yours today."
**With Sugarman's technique:** "My back surrendered first. Then my focus. I figured the problem was my chair — so I spent $900 replacing it, and nothing changed. That's when a physical therapist told me the one thing no chair can fix."

## Best for
Long-form sales pages, product launch emails, story-driven blog posts, newsletters, webinar and speech openings.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is the first sentence under ~8 words and impossible not to follow?
- [ ] Does every paragraph end with a pull into the next — no dead stops?
- [ ] Are curiosity bridges planted at each natural exit point, each with a payoff that arrives?
- [ ] Is there one big concept the piece sells, with features as evidence rather than a list?
- [ ] Do the opening lines earn several silent "yes" nods before the first contested claim?
- [ ] Are the reader's top objections voiced and answered at the moment they'd arise?
