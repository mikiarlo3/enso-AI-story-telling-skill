# Gary Halbert — Personal, One-to-One Direct Mail Voice

The "Prince of Print," a direct-mail legend whose sales letters read like urgent notes from a friend. His philosophy: markets and human connection beat clever writing — talk to one real person about something they already desperately want.

## Pass the A-pile test
Halbert observed that people sort mail into an A-pile (personal — always opened) and a B-pile (promotional — often trashed). Writing that looks and sounds personal gets read; writing that smells like marketing gets filtered out before a single argument lands.
**When to use:** Emails, newsletters, LinkedIn posts, outreach — anywhere the reader triages fast.
**Apply it in prose:** Strip the trappings of "content": no corporate greetings, no stock phrases, no design-speak. Open the way a smart friend would open a note about something urgent. If the first two lines could appear in any company's campaign, rewrite them until they could only come from you.

## Write to one perfect reader
A letter aimed at "everyone" connects with no one, because the language goes generic to cover all cases. Writing to one vividly imagined person forces concrete details, second-person address, and natural voice — and every reader feels personally spoken to.
**When to use:** Any persuasive or personal-voice piece.
**Apply it in prose:** Before drafting, sketch one specific reader — their situation, frustration, and what they did yesterday about this problem. Write the whole piece as a message to that one person, using "you" throughout. Cut any sentence that person would skim.

## Find the starving crowd first
Asked what advantage he'd want in a hamburger-selling contest, Halbert's answer was a starving crowd — not a better burger or better copy. Market hunger comes first, the offer second, copy third, because no wording rescues a message aimed at people who don't already want the thing.
**When to use:** Before writing anything — topic selection, campaign planning, content strategy.
**Apply it in prose:** Before drafting, name the existing hunger this piece feeds: what is this audience already searching for, complaining about, or paying for? If you can't name it, change the topic or the audience before polishing a sentence. Lead with the hunger, not with your product.

## Conversational voice with momentum
Halbert wrote the way people talk — short sentences, contractions, direct address, quick asides — because conversational rhythm keeps the eye moving while formal prose invites skimming. Momentum is engineered: varied sentence lengths, paragraph breaks at moments of tension, transitions that lean forward.
**When to use:** Long emails, sales pages, story-driven posts.
**Apply it in prose:** Read the draft aloud; rewrite anything you'd never say. Break paragraphs at two or three sentences. End some paragraphs mid-thought so the next one must be read. Use contractions and plain words everywhere formality has crept in.

## The first paragraph has one job
The opening paragraph exists solely to get the second paragraph read — not to summarize, credential, or set context. Front-loaded throat-clearing kills the read before the argument starts, because attention is at its most fragile in the first seconds.
**When to use:** Every piece, ruthlessly.
**Apply it in prose:** Delete your draft's first paragraph and see if the piece gets stronger — it usually does. Open with the most gripping thing you have: a startling fact, a mid-scene moment, a blunt statement of the reader's problem. Save background for after the hook has set.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "We are excited to announce our new financial planning webinar series for professionals seeking to optimize their retirement strategies."
**With Halbert's technique:** "Last Tuesday a 58-year-old engineer showed me his retirement account and asked if he could still fix it. I want to show you exactly what I told him."

## Best for
Email marketing, newsletters, sales letters, founder-voice LinkedIn posts, cold outreach, fundraising appeals.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Would this land in the reader's mental A-pile — does it read as personal, not promotional?
- [ ] Is it written to one specific person, in second person, in words I'd actually say aloud?
- [ ] Have I named the existing hunger this piece feeds, before polishing the copy?
- [ ] Does the first paragraph do nothing but compel the second?
- [ ] Are paragraphs short, with varied sentence rhythm that keeps the eye falling forward?
- [ ] Did I try deleting the opening paragraph to see if the piece improves?
