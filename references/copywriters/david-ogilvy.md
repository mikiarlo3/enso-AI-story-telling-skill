# David Ogilvy — Research-Driven Persuasion & Headlines

Founder of Ogilvy & Mather and the most quoted advertising man of the 20th century. His philosophy: advertising exists to sell, and what sells is discovered through research, delivered with respect for the reader's intelligence.

## Spend 80 cents on the headline
Roughly five times as many people read a headline as read what follows it, so the headline carries most of the persuasive load — if it fails, the rest of the writing never gets a chance. Ogilvy framed this as spending eighty cents of your dollar on the headline.
**When to use:** Every titled piece — blog posts, email subject lines, LinkedIn hooks, landing pages.
**Apply it in prose:** Draft the body, then write 10–20 headline candidates before choosing one. Put the reader's benefit or a genuinely new piece of information in the headline itself, not a teaser for it. Judge the headline as if it were the only line anyone will read — because for most readers, it is.

## Specificity sells
Concrete facts and numbers are believed; adjectives are discounted as advertising noise. A precise claim ("cuts onboarding from 3 weeks to 4 days") does the persuading that "dramatically faster" only gestures at, because specifics imply the writer actually measured something.
**When to use:** Any claim of quality, speed, size, or results.
**Apply it in prose:** Hunt every adjective in your draft ("powerful," "seamless," "world-class") and replace it with the fact that made you want to write it. If no fact exists, cut the claim rather than pad it.

## Long copy works — if every line earns its place
Ogilvy found that people who are genuinely in the market read long, detailed copy eagerly; length only fails when it's boring. The real rule isn't "keep it short," it's "keep it interesting," because an engaged prospect wants more information, not less.
**When to use:** High-consideration decisions — B2B services, expensive products, career or health topics.
**Apply it in prose:** Don't cut for length; cut for dullness. Read each paragraph asking "would a genuinely interested reader want this fact?" Keep everything that answers a real buyer question; delete everything that's throat-clearing.

## Research until the big idea emerges
Ogilvy studied a product, its makers, and its customers exhaustively before writing a word — his most famous campaigns came from details buried in technical documents. The big idea is found, not invented, because the most persuasive angle is usually a true fact nobody has dramatized yet.
**When to use:** Before drafting anything about a product, company, or person.
**Apply it in prose:** Before writing, list 20 true facts about the subject — specs, origins, process details, customer quotes. Pick the single most surprising true fact and build the piece around it instead of opening with a generic claim.

## Respect the reader's intelligence
Ogilvy's line "the consumer is not a moron; she is your wife" was a warning against condescension: readers detect exaggeration, tricks, and empty cleverness, and they punish it with distrust. Writing that treats the reader as a smart adult earns the credibility that makes claims land.
**When to use:** Always — especially in marketing copy tempted toward hype.
**Apply it in prose:** Delete hype words, false urgency, and jokes that mock the reader's problem. Give real information a smart skeptic would find useful. Ask of every sentence: would I say this, straight-faced, to an intelligent friend?

## Story appeal in the opening
Ogilvy injected narrative curiosity — an unexplained detail that begs a question — into images and openings (the eyepatch on the Hathaway shirt man is the classic case). An open story loop pulls readers in because the mind demands resolution of a mystery it has noticed.
**When to use:** Openings of posts, newsletters, speeches; any piece fighting for attention.
**Apply it in prose:** Open with a specific, slightly odd concrete detail that raises a question you don't immediately answer — a scene, an object, a decision that seems wrong. Resolve it a few paragraphs in, once the reader is invested.

## Before / after
**Flat:** "Our project management software is powerful and easy to use. Try it today."
**With Ogilvy's technique:** "At 60 users, the average team wastes 11 hours a week in status meetings. Ours run our whole 400-person company on zero."

## Best for
Landing pages, long-form blog posts, B2B marketing, product announcements, any high-consideration purchase content, email subject lines.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Did I write at least 10 headline options and pick the most benefit-loaded one?
- [ ] Is every adjective replaced by (or backed by) a specific fact or number?
- [ ] Does the opening contain a concrete, curiosity-raising detail?
- [ ] Would an intelligent skeptic find real information here, not hype?
- [ ] Is the length justified by interest — no dull paragraph kept out of habit?
- [ ] Is the piece built around the most surprising true fact I found in research?
