# Ernest Hemingway — Compression & Omission

The novelist and former reporter who stripped English prose to its load-bearing walls. His philosophy: the power of a story lives in what you leave out — provided you, the writer, know exactly what you're leaving out.

## The iceberg theory
Hemingway argued (in Death in the Afternoon) that a writer who knows their subject can omit most of it, and the omitted part strengthens the story — the reader feels the seven-eighths below the surface. It works because readers sense the difference between a gap and a hollow: omission from knowledge radiates weight; omission from ignorance radiates thinness.
**When to use:** Emotional or high-stakes material — layoff announcements, failure stories, grief, hard pivots.
**Apply it in prose:** Write down everything you know about the situation, then publish only the visible tip: the concrete facts and actions. Cut the explanations of how it felt. "We told the team on a Tuesday. Four people had started that month." Do not add "it was heartbreaking" — the reader supplies that, and their version is stronger than yours.

## Short declarative sentences carry emotion
Hemingway delivered his heaviest moments in his plainest sentences. It works because ornate prose spends the reader's attention on the writing; plain prose spends it on the thing — and at emotional peaks, any visible effort reads as manipulation.
**When to use:** The most important two or three sentences in any piece: the reveal, the loss, the decision, the ending.
**Apply it in prose:** Find your piece's peak moment and rewrite it as short declaratives — subject, verb, object, no adverbs, no metaphors. Save complexity for low-stakes connective passages. Rule of thumb: the more the moment matters, the shorter the sentences that carry it.

## Dialogue that hides more than it says
Hemingway's characters talk around the real subject — in "Hills Like White Elephants" a couple discusses drinks and scenery while deciding something neither will name. Subtext works because eavesdropping on what people won't say makes readers active participants; on-the-nose dialogue makes them spectators.
**When to use:** Case studies and narratives that quote real conversations — customer calls, board meetings, exit interviews.
**Apply it in prose:** When quoting people, keep the lines that dodge, deflect, or understate — those reveal more than clean summary quotes. "The customer said, 'No, it's fine. We'll manage.' They churned three weeks later." Resist the urge to explain what the quote really meant.

## Write the truest sentence you know, and start there
Hemingway's remedy for being stuck (described in A Moveable Feast): write one true sentence — the truest one you know about the subject — and continue from there. It works because a true, simple statement cuts through the throat-clearing that openings accumulate, and it anchors everything after it in honesty.
**When to use:** Blank-page paralysis; drafts whose openings feel like PR; any piece that starts with three paragraphs of wind-up.
**Apply it in prose:** Ask: what is the truest single sentence about this topic — the one you'd say to a friend off the record? Write it down plainly ("Most of our growth last year came from one customer"). Make it the first line, or place it early, and delete whatever wind-up used to sit before it.

## Stop while it's going well
Hemingway ended each writing day mid-flow, while he still knew what came next — never writing himself dry. Applied to the reader's side: end scenes and sections before they're exhausted, because a section that stops at its peak leaves momentum, while a section that empties itself leaves fatigue.
**When to use:** Section endings, newsletter installments, chapter breaks in long content, and the ending of the whole piece.
**Apply it in prose:** End each section one beat earlier than feels complete — after the strong line, before the summary of the strong line. Cut wrap-up sentences that restate what the section just showed. If a section ends with "In short, ..." delete that sentence and see if the ending improved. It almost always did.

## Before / after
**Flat:** Shutting down our first product was an incredibly emotional and difficult decision that taught us many painful but valuable lessons about listening to the market.
**With Hemingway's technique:** We shut the product down on a Friday. It had taken two years to build and eleven minutes to turn off. Forty-two customers emailed. We answered every one.

## Best for
Failure stories and post-mortems, sensitive announcements, personal essays, executive communications, any piece where restraint reads as strength and the audience is tired of being sold to.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is the emotional weight carried by omitted material you actually know, not by adjectives?
- [ ] Are the highest-stakes moments written in the shortest sentences?
- [ ] Do quoted conversations keep their subtext instead of being explained?
- [ ] Is the truest sentence you know placed at or near the top?
- [ ] Does each section end one beat before exhaustion, with no restating wrap-up?
