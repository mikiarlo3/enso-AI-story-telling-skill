# Stephen King — Situation, Honesty & the Merciless Second Draft

The bestselling novelist whose craft memoir On Writing became a standard manual far beyond horror. His philosophy: put believable people in a compelling situation, tell the truth about what they'd do, then cut everything that isn't the story.

## Situation over plot
King distrusts plotting. He starts with a "what if?" situation — what if a bullied girl had telekinesis? — drops honest characters into it, and lets events follow from who they are. It works because readers smell contrivance: outcomes that grow from character feel inevitable, while outcomes arranged by the author feel like being handled.
**When to use:** Case studies, scenario-driven marketing, thought-leadership built on a hypothetical.
**Apply it in prose:** Frame content as a situation, not a thesis. Instead of "5 reasons remote onboarding fails," try "A new hire logs on at 9 a.m. Monday. Nobody has told her team she exists. What happens next?" Then follow the situation honestly — including where it makes your product or argument look imperfect, because that honesty is what makes the rest believable.

## Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open
King drafts for himself alone — door closed, no imagined critics — then revises for the reader, door open, asking what an outside person needs. Separating the modes works because drafting and judging use opposing instincts; running both at once produces slow, timid prose.
**When to use:** Every piece, but especially ones you're anxious about — sensitive topics, big audiences, first posts.
**Apply it in prose:** Draft fast and privately: no rereading, no imagining the comments section, opinions at full strength. Then, in a separate pass, open the door: cut inside references, define jargon, soften only what's genuinely unfair, and check that a stranger can follow every leap. Never mix the two passes.

## Kill your darlings
The advice King made famous in On Writing (the phrase descends from Arthur Quiller-Couch's "murder your darlings"): cut the passages you're proudest of if they don't serve the story. It works because darling passages exist to please the writer, and readers can feel a paragraph performing for its author instead of for them.
**When to use:** Second-draft editing, always. Especially clever titles, extended metaphors, and jokes you've saved for weeks.
**Apply it in prose:** In revision, flag the three passages you'd be saddest to lose. For each, ask: if a colleague had written this, would I tell them to cut it? Anything kept purely because it's clever, goes. Paste darlings into a "later" file so deletion costs less — most never earn their way back.

## The reader writes half the horror
King's monsters are scariest before they're shown; the closed door frightens more than whatever's behind it, because the reader's imagination customizes the threat to their own fears. Implication beats description whenever the audience's version of the stakes is more vivid than any version you could specify.
**When to use:** Risk, urgency, and loss-framing in marketing; describing failure consequences; building suspense in narratives.
**Apply it in prose:** Name the threat's evidence, not the threat's face. "Three of your competitors have already quietly rebuilt their pricing" beats a paragraph cataloguing doom. Give one concrete detail that proves the danger is real, then stop and let the reader's imagination — which knows their fears better than you do — finish the job.

## Second draft = first draft minus 10%
King's formula, taped above his desk since a rejection-slip note early in his career: the second draft should be at least ten percent shorter than the first. A hard number works where "be concise" fails, because it forces line-level choices — and the cuts almost always remove hedges, wind-ups, and repetition the writer couldn't otherwise see.
**When to use:** Every revision pass on every piece, from tweets to whitepapers.
**Apply it in prose:** Check the word count after drafting; set a target of 90% or less. Cut in this order: throat-clearing openings, qualifiers ("very," "quite," "I think"), repeated points, and explanations of things you already showed. If the piece was 1,000 words, ship at 900 or fewer — and notice that nothing you cut is missed.

## Hunt the adverbs
King's toolbox rule from On Writing: the adverb is not your friend — "the road to hell is paved with adverbs." Adverbs work against you because each one confesses that the verb it props up was too weak; a strong verb carries the manner inside itself and reads twice as fast.
**When to use:** Line editing on every piece; especially marketing copy, where "-ly" words pile up ("seamlessly," "effortlessly," "significantly").
**Apply it in prose:** Search the draft for "ly " and judge each hit. "Walked quickly" becomes "strode"; "grew significantly" becomes "tripled"; "seamlessly integrates" becomes "connects in one click." Keep an adverb only when no verb exists that contains it. Apply the same suspicion to dialogue tags — "said" needs no decoration.

## Before / after
**Flat:** Cybersecurity threats are increasing rapidly and could have very serious consequences for businesses of all sizes, which is why investing in protection is extremely important in today's landscape.
**With King's technique:** The email looked like it came from the CFO. It didn't. By the time anyone checked, the wire had cleared — on a Tuesday, at a 40-person company that believed it was too small to target. Nobody there knows yet. That part comes later.

## Best for
Scenario-driven marketing, risk and security content, case studies, suspenseful long-form narratives, and as an editing discipline layered onto any other storyteller's structure.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Is the piece built on a "what if" situation followed honestly, not a thesis with arranged evidence?
- [ ] Were drafting and editing done as fully separate passes?
- [ ] Have the passages you're proudest of been tested for whether they serve the reader?
- [ ] Are the stakes implied through evidence, leaving the reader to imagine the worst?
- [ ] Is the final draft at least 10% shorter than the first?
- [ ] Has every adverb been tested against a stronger verb?
