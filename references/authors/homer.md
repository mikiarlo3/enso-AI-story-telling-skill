# Homer — Epic Structure & Memory Hooks

The poet (or tradition) behind the Iliad and Odyssey, composed for live oral performance where nothing could be re-read. The philosophy: build the story so it can survive in memory — the audience's and the teller's.

## In medias res
The Odyssey doesn't start with Odysseus leaving Troy; it starts ten years later, with him stranded and his house overrun, then loops back to fill in the journey. Starting in the middle of the trouble works because a question ("how did it get this bad?") pulls harder than a chronology.
**When to use:** Case studies, origin stories, project retrospectives — anything where the chronological beginning is the least interesting part.
**Apply it in prose:** Open at the worst or strangest moment: "Eighteen months in, we had four customers and three of them were friends of the founder." Then explicitly rewind — "It didn't start that way" — and tell the backstory the reader now wants.

## The invocation — the whole story in miniature
Homer's openings ask the Muse to sing of one thing — the rage of Achilles, the man of many turns — and in a few lines sketch the entire arc to come. It works as a contract: the audience knows what kind of story this is and what it's about, so every episode reads as progress toward a promised destination.
**When to use:** Long posts, reports, talks, email series — anything over ~800 words.
**Apply it in prose:** After your hook, write a two-to-three-sentence miniature of the whole piece: the subject, the struggle, and a hint of the outcome without spoiling how. "This is the story of how one spreadsheet nearly killed the company — and why the fix wasn't software." Then deliver exactly that story.

## Epithets and formula as memory hooks
Homer brands people and things with repeated tags: swift-footed Achilles, wine-dark sea, rosy-fingered dawn. Repetition of a vivid fixed phrase works because it turns a character into a handle the mind can grab — each reuse reinforces identity instead of boring the reader.
**When to use:** Recurring characters, products, or concepts across a long piece or a content series.
**Apply it in prose:** Coin one short, concrete tag per key player and reuse it verbatim: "the spreadsheet that ran the company," "our quietest customer," "the Tuesday deploy." Never vary the wording for elegance — the sameness is the point. Two or three tags per piece, maximum.

## The episodic quest with one burning goal
Odysseus faces a cyclops, a witch, sirens, a whirlpool — wildly different episodes — but every one is measured against a single unchanging goal: get home. Variety keeps attention; the fixed goal keeps meaning, because each episode can be judged as progress or setback on the same axis.
**When to use:** Multi-part narratives, year-in-review posts, long case studies, newsletter serials.
**Apply it in prose:** State the single goal early and never change it. Let each section be a different kind of obstacle (technical, human, financial), and end each one by marking distance to the goal: closer, farther, or transformed. If a section doesn't touch the goal, cut it.

## Catalogs made vivid
Homer's Catalog of Ships lists dozens of contingents, but each entry gets its own concrete flourish — a hometown, a father, a claim to fame — so the list becomes a parade rather than an inventory. Lists die when items are abstract; one specific detail per item keeps each alive.
**When to use:** Feature lists, team introductions, roundups, "lessons learned" listicles.
**Apply it in prose:** Never list bare items. Give every bullet one concrete, differentiating detail: not "improved onboarding" but "onboarding rebuilt after we watched a customer give up on step 3, twice." If you can't find a distinct detail for an item, merge it with another or delete it.

## Ring composition — the digression that returns
Homer nests stories inside stories — the scar of Odysseus triggers a long flashback to a boyhood boar hunt — but every digression closes its loop and returns precisely to the frame. It works because a digression that returns feels like depth, while one that doesn't feels like losing the thread; the return is what retroactively justifies the detour.
**When to use:** Long-form pieces that need backstory, definitions, or side-cases without derailing the main narrative.
**Apply it in prose:** When you digress, mark the departure point with a concrete image ("the whiteboard still said 'ship Friday'"), tell the side-story completely, then land back on that exact image before continuing. Never open a second digression inside the first.

## Before / after
**Flat:** In this post I'll cover our company's journey over five years, including the challenges we faced and the lessons we learned along the way.
**With Homer's technique:** The acquisition offer arrived on the same day our biggest customer churned. To explain how both happened in one morning, I have to rewind five years — to a two-person team with one goal we never changed: make invoicing invisible. Everything that follows, cyclops included, was measured against that.

## Best for
Long-form case studies, company origin stories, newsletter serials, annual reviews, conference talks, any roundup or list-heavy piece that risks reading like inventory.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Does the piece open mid-trouble, with the chronological start deferred?
- [ ] Is there a short up-front miniature promising the whole arc?
- [ ] Do key players carry a repeated, concrete tag used verbatim?
- [ ] Is there one fixed goal every section measures itself against?
- [ ] Does every list item carry its own specific, differentiating detail?
- [ ] Does every digression close its loop and return to the exact point it left?
