# Kurt Vonnegut — Story Shapes & Ruthless Economy

The novelist whose rejected master's thesis — that stories have shapes you can graph — became his most famous lecture. His philosophy: know your story's shape before you write it, and make every sentence earn its place twice.

## Graph fortune over time before drafting
Vonnegut plotted stories on two axes: good fortune to ill fortune (vertical), beginning to end (horizontal). Sketching the curve first works because it exposes structural problems — a flat line, a shapeless wobble — before you've spent hours on sentences, and because readers experience a story *as* that curve whether you planned it or not.
**When to use:** Before drafting any narrative content: case study, founder story, campaign arc, keynote.
**Apply it in prose:** Before writing, sketch the protagonist's fortune line across your piece in ten seconds. If it's flat, you have a report, not a story — find the dip or the rise, or reframe around whoever's fortune actually moved. Check each section against the curve: is fortune rising or falling here, and does the reader feel it?

## The shapes: Man in a Hole, Boy Meets Girl, Cinderella
Three of Vonnegut's canonical curves, each a reusable template because audiences recognize them instantly and lean into the expected motion:

```
Man in a Hole            Boy Meets Girl           Cinderella
(end better than start)  (find, lose, regain)     (rise, crash, transcend)

good ──╮      ╭── ✓      good   ╭──╮    ╭── ✓     good            ╭─ ✓ (off the chart)
        ╲    ╱                 ╱    ╲  ╱                  ╭─╮_╭─╮ │
         ╲__╱            ── ──╯      ╲╱            ______╱      ╲╯
ill                      ill                       ill        (midnight)
```

- **Man in a Hole:** ordinary bearable life → misfortune strikes → climb out, ending *better off than before*. The workhorse.
- **Boy Meets Girl:** ordinary day → find something wonderful → lose it → win it back for good.
- **Cinderella:** low start → incremental staircase of gains → sudden catastrophic loss (midnight) → restoration beyond the previous peak.

**When to use:** Choosing a structure for any narrative; diagnosing why an existing draft feels shapeless.
**Apply it in prose — which shape for which content:**
- *Man in a Hole* → case studies, failure-lesson posts, post-mortems, "how we fixed it" content. (Customer had a working setup, disaster hit, your solution left them stronger than before the disaster.)
- *Boy Meets Girl* → product love stories, churn-and-win-back narratives, career essays about leaving and returning to a calling, rebrand stories.
- *Cinderella* → founder journeys, transformation testimonials, underdog brand narratives, funding announcements with a near-death chapter. The midnight crash is mandatory — a pure staircase up is a brag, not a story.

## Every character should want something
One of Vonnegut's rules for fiction: every character should want something, even if it's only a glass of water. Stated desire works because a want creates a question (will they get it?) and a direction — a character who wants nothing gives the reader nothing to root for or measure events against.
**When to use:** Any content featuring people: customers in case studies, teams in retros, the narrator of a personal essay, even "the reader" in how-to content.
**Apply it in prose:** Give every person who appears a stated, concrete want within a sentence of introducing them — "The ops lead wanted one thing: to stop being paged at 3 a.m." Small, specific wants beat grand abstract ones. If someone in your piece wants nothing, either give them a want or cut them.

## Start as close to the end as possible
Another Vonnegut rule: begin the story near its climax, not at the chronological start. It works because everything before the trouble is prologue the reader must take on faith, while everything near the end is charged — and readers extend patience only after they're invested.
**When to use:** Openings of everything, especially pieces whose drafts begin with background or company history.
**Apply it in prose:** Find the latest possible entry point that still makes sense: not "We founded the company in 2019" but "The night before the launch, our payment provider dropped us." Delete your draft's first paragraphs until you hit the first sentence with tension in it; start there and backfill only what's essential.

## Be a sadist to your protagonist
Vonnegut's bluntest rule: no matter how sweet your leading characters, make awful things happen to them — so the reader can see what they are made of. It works because character is revealed only under pressure; a protagonist who is never truly tested gives the audience no evidence for admiring them.
**When to use:** Case studies and brand stories that are drifting into unbroken success; testimonial narratives.
**Apply it in prose:** Do not sand off the disasters. Include the moment the migration failed, the champion who quit mid-project, the quarter the numbers went backwards — in concrete detail, at real severity. The impressiveness of the recovery is exactly proportional to the awfulness you were willing to show.

## Every sentence must do double duty
Vonnegut held that every sentence should reveal character or advance the action — ideally both. As an editing standard it works because it gives you a binary test for each line, and prose in which every sentence moves something is prose readers describe as "unputdownable" without knowing why.
**When to use:** Final editing pass on any narrative piece.
**Apply it in prose:** Read each sentence and ask two questions: does this move events forward, or does it show who someone (or the company, or the product) really is? Sentences that only decorate, restate, or hedge — cut. Scene-setting survives only if it does character work ("the office plants had died in March; nobody had noticed until the acquirer's tour").

## Before / after
**Flat:** Acme Corp adopted our platform in 2022 and has since seen significant improvements in operational efficiency across multiple departments and metrics.
**With Vonnegut's technique:** By the time Acme called us, their ops lead had one want: a single week without a 3 a.m. page. They'd been climbing out of that hole for a year — new tools, new runbooks — and then the Black Friday outage knocked them back to the bottom. That's where we came in. This year, Black Friday broke their sales record. The pager stayed silent.

## Best for
Case studies, founder and transformation stories, failure-lesson posts, testimonial narratives, campaign arcs — and as a pre-writing planning tool for any content with people in it.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Did you sketch the fortune-over-time curve before drafting, and does the draft match it?
- [ ] Is the shape deliberate (Man in a Hole / Boy Meets Girl / Cinderella) and suited to the content type?
- [ ] Does every person in the piece want something specific?
- [ ] Does the piece start as close to the climax as sense allows?
- [ ] Do genuinely bad things happen to the protagonist, shown at real severity?
- [ ] Does every sentence reveal character or advance the action?
