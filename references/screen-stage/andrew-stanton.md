# Andrew Stanton — Earned Investment & the Working Reader

Writer-director of Finding Nemo and WALL-E and co-writer on Pixar's founding films, Andrew Stanton laid out his story doctrine in a famous 2012 TED talk. His philosophy: storytelling is joke-telling — everything you write is in service of a single punchline the audience must be made to care about reaching.

## Make me care
Stanton's first commandment: the audience's silent demand of every story is "make me care" — emotionally, intellectually, aesthetically — and it must be answered immediately. Attention is lent, not owed; a piece that delays giving a reason to care is a piece already skimmed.
**When to use:** Every opening, of everything.
**Apply it in prose:** Within the first three sentences, give the reader a stake: a tension they recognize, a cost they fear, a question they need answered, or a person they instantly feel for. Test your opening by asking what a busy stranger gets for continuing — if the honest answer is "context," rewrite it.

## The opening promise
Stanton compares a well-told beginning to a pebble pulled back in a slingshot: a good opening makes a promise that this will lead somewhere worth your time, and stored tension launches everything after. Readers grant patience in exchange for a credible promise, not for quality alone.
**When to use:** First paragraphs of posts and emails; first minute of talks; subject lines and hooks.
**Apply it in prose:** Make the promise concrete and slightly tensioned: "By the end of this, you'll know why we refund customers who don't ask" out-pulls a topic statement. Then honor it precisely — an opening promise the ending doesn't pay off costs you the next piece's trust.

## The 2+2 rule
Stanton's "unifying theory of 2+2": give the audience two plus two, never four — well-organized absence of information draws readers in, and they'll work for their meal because concluding for themselves creates ownership of the meaning. What a reader assembles, a reader believes.
**When to use:** Endings, punchlines, persuasion pieces, anywhere you're tempted to write "the lesson here is…".
**Apply it in prose:** Lay the pieces in the right order, then cut the sentence that adds them up. "Our churn was 9%. The competitor we mocked in every all-hands just crossed 50,000 users" needs no "we had underestimated them." Delete stated conclusions the reader can reach in one step; keep only conclusions that need more than one step of inference.

## Character spine
Stanton says every well-drawn character runs on a spine — an inner motor, an itch they can't scratch, that drives everything they do (Nemo's father's spine is to prevent harm; WALL-E's is to find beauty). One dominant drive makes behavior coherent and predictions possible, which is what makes a character feel real.
**When to use:** Founder stories, brand voice, case study subjects, recurring narrators.
**Apply it in prose:** Give every central figure — including a brand narrating its own content — one stated or shown driving itch: "she couldn't leave an inefficient process alone," "we can't ship anything we wouldn't use daily." Filter their actions in the piece through that spine, and let their key decision at the climax express it.

## Know your ending, write toward one destination
Stanton's craft rule: know your punchline before you start, because a story is ultimately one aimed telling — everything included should be pointing to that landing. A known destination is your editing criterion; without it, every paragraph is arguably relevant and nothing is cuttable.
**When to use:** Before drafting; during every structural edit.
**Apply it in prose:** Write the destination sentence first — the single realization the reader should end on. Then audit every section: does it move the reader toward that sentence? Cut or relocate anything aimed elsewhere, however good. One piece, one landing.

## Use what you know
Stanton's counterintuitive reading of "write what you know": it doesn't mean plot from your life — it means capture a truth from your lived experience of it, express values you personally feel deep down. Borrowed insight reads as summary; lived insight carries detail and conviction no research can fake.
**When to use:** Thought leadership, opinion pieces, personal essays, brand values content.
**Apply it in prose:** Anchor every major claim in something actually experienced — a real conversation, a real failure, a real Tuesday — even when the topic is abstract. If you (or the brand) have no lived contact with the point, either get some, interview someone who does, or cut the point. Never dress secondhand takes as testimony.

## Leave room for wonder
Stanton prizes stories that invite wonder — the audience's own imagination completing what the storyteller deliberately leaves open. Total closure ends the reader's relationship with a piece; a well-chosen open edge keeps them thinking, and a reader still thinking is a reader who shares.
**When to use:** Endings; big-vision sections; pieces meant to provoke discussion.
**Apply it in prose:** After paying off your promise, leave one genuine opening: an honest unresolved question, an implication you decline to spell out, a "what would this mean for your team?" that isn't rhetorical. Don't manufacture false mystery — leave open only what is truly still open.

## Before / after
**Flat:** In conclusion, this experience taught us that listening to customers is very important, and we recommend that all companies prioritize customer feedback.
**With Stanton's technique:** We spent $30,000 on a feature nobody used. The fix that revived our retention came from a support ticket we'd marked "low priority" eight months earlier. We've changed how we read our inbox. What's sitting in yours?

## Best for
Openings and endings of everything, persuasion and thought-leadership pieces, personal essays, talks and keynotes, tightening drafts that over-explain.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Does the reader have a reason to care within the first three sentences?
- [ ] Does the opening make a concrete promise the ending precisely pays off?
- [ ] Have I cut every conclusion the reader can assemble themselves — 2+2, not 4?
- [ ] Does each central figure have one driving itch that their actions express?
- [ ] Did I write the destination sentence first, and does every section aim at it?
- [ ] Is at least one major claim anchored in lived experience rather than research?
