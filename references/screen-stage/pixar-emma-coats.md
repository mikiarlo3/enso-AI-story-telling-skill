# Emma Coats — Pixar's Rules of Story Craft

Emma Coats is the former Pixar story artist whose tweeted "22 rules of storytelling," distilled from the studio's story-room practice, became the most-shared story craft checklist in modern culture. Her philosophy: story is a craft of causality and sacrifice — every beat must earn the next, and the writer must give up what they love for what the audience needs.

## The story spine
A fill-in-the-blank causality skeleton — "Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…" — originated by playwright Kenn Adams and popularized as one of Coats's rules. It works because it forces every event to be caused by the previous one, converting a list of things that happened into a chain the reader must follow.
**When to use:** Any narrative: case studies, founder stories, incident writeups, even explanations of how a situation arose.
**Apply it in prose:** Draft the skeleton first: status quo ("every day, the team exported reports by hand"), disruption ("until one day a client asked for hourly data"), consequences chained with because ("because of that, we tried X; because of that, Y broke"), resolution ("until finally…"). If any beat connects with "and then" instead of "because of that," you have a list, not a story — fix the causality.

## Coincidence in, never out
Coats's rule: coincidences that get characters into trouble are great; coincidences that get them out of it are cheating. Readers forgive lucky problems but resent lucky solutions, because an unearned resolution retroactively deletes the tension they invested in.
**When to use:** Case studies, success stories, any narrative with a resolution.
**Apply it in prose:** Your inciting problem can arrive by chance ("a random audit flagged us"), but the resolution must come from deliberate action by the people in the story. If the real ending involved luck ("a big client happened to sign that week"), be honest about the luck and foreground the decisions that let you capitalize on it — earned agency is the story.

## Simplify ruthlessly
Coats's rule pairs simplify and focus: combine characters, hop over detours — it feels like losing valuable material, but it sets the piece free. Every extra thread taxes the reader's working memory, so each cut concentrates attention on what remains.
**When to use:** Every revision pass, especially on drafts that "cover a lot."
**Apply it in prose:** Merge similar examples into one vivid one; collapse three minor stakeholders into the single person the reader needs to track; delete tangents into a future post's idea file. Test each element: if removing it doesn't weaken the spine, remove it. Expect the cut to hurt — that pain is not evidence the material should stay.

## Solve your ending first
Coats's rule: work out your ending before you write your middle — endings are hard, so get them working up front. A known destination disciplines every earlier choice, because each beat can be judged by whether it moves toward the landing.
**When to use:** Before drafting anything with an argument or arc.
**Apply it in prose:** Write your final paragraph — or at least the final takeaway sentence — first. Then build the middle as the shortest honest path there. If you can't state the ending, you're not ready to draft; you're still researching.

## Engineer the stakes
Coats's rules demand you give the audience reasons to root for the character and show what happens if they fail. Investment requires a rooting interest plus a visible cost, because readers lean in only when an outcome is both desirable and genuinely in doubt.
**When to use:** Openings of case studies and narratives; any persuasion piece.
**Apply it in prose:** Early on, establish both sides of the wager: what the people in the piece are trying to win and what failure concretely costs ("miss the deadline and the funding milestone dies with it"). For argument pieces, stake the reader: what do they lose by getting this wrong? No stakes stated, no tension available.

## Admire effort over success
Coats's rule: why must you tell this story? Related: audiences admire a character for trying more than for succeeding. Struggle is more bonding than victory, because effort is universal while success is circumstantial — readers see themselves in the attempt.
**When to use:** Founder stories, personal essays, brand narratives, retrospectives.
**Apply it in prose:** Spend more words on the attempts — including failed ones — than on the trophy. "We rewrote the onboarding flow four times; the third version tested worse than the original" earns more trust and connection than the final metric alone. Show the reader the trying.

## The audience's fun, not yours
Coats's rule: what's interesting to you as a writer can be very different from what's interesting to the audience — and they can't be bribed with your enthusiasm. Attention follows the reader's needs, so self-indulgent material reads as padding no matter how much you enjoyed writing it.
**When to use:** Every revision; especially technical deep-dives and personal essays.
**Apply it in prose:** For each section, ask who it serves. The clever architecture detail, the inside joke, the paragraph you're proudest of — if it serves your enjoyment rather than the reader's goal, cut it or earn it by making it do reader-facing work. Kill your darlings on the reader's behalf, not as ritual.

## The essence question
Coats's deepest rule: why must you tell this story? What burns in you that this piece feeds? That belief is the heart of it. A piece with a discoverable "why" has a spine of conviction the reader can feel, while topic-shaped content without one reads as filler.
**When to use:** Before committing to write anything; when a draft feels hollow.
**Apply it in prose:** Before drafting, answer in one sentence: why must this piece exist, and why must it come from you (or this brand) specifically? If the honest answer is "the content calendar," find the angle only you can supply — the data only you have, the failure only you lived — or don't write it.

## Before / after
**Flat:** We tried several growth strategies last year and eventually found one that worked, which helped us grow significantly. Here are some lessons.
**With Coats's technique:** Every month we ran the same playbook: paid ads, break even, repeat. Until one day the ad account got suspended — three weeks before a funding milestone that required 20% growth. Because we couldn't buy traffic, we emailed our 400 existing users personally. Because forty of them replied with the same complaint, we finally saw the referral blocker we'd been paying to ignore. Fixing it did what $60k of ads never had.

## Best for
Case studies, founder and personal narratives, incident retrospectives, structural editing of any draft that rambles, deciding what to write at all.

## Draft checklist
- [ ] Do the beats connect with "because of that," never just "and then"?
- [ ] Does the resolution come from earned action, with any luck honestly framed?
- [ ] Have I combined redundant examples and cut detours, even ones I love?
- [ ] Did I know the ending before I wrote the middle?
- [ ] Are the stakes explicit — what's being fought for and what failure costs?
- [ ] Can I answer why this piece must exist and why it must come from me?
